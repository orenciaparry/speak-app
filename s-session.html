The S Sound Session

A 30-minute practice session for Elijah, 7, whose /s/ comes out like "th" because the tongue tip slips between the teeth. It follows the standard articulation ladder therapists use, one rung at a time, and is meant to run alongside his speech therapy, at the level his therapist has set.

30 min · can be split 2 × 15target: 80–100 triesmove up at 80% correct4–5 sessions a week

The cue card

Say the same four words every time. The whole session hangs on these, and the app and the games are just ways to rehearse them.

The
S
sound
  1. Teeth together — gently, like a smile with the teeth closed
  2. Tongue in — tip rests behind the bottom teeth, or up behind the bumpy ridge if the therapist prefers
  3. Big smile — lips pulled back, not rounded
  4. Blow the S — thin cold air down the middle: sssss

Before you start

Gather

  • A wooden coffee stirrer or a lolly stick (the "tongue fence").
  • A drinking straw.
  • The word cards below, or the pictures on the tablet.
  • A counter game: any board game, a jar and 20 marbles, or a tower of blocks. One move per 5 correct tries.
  • The tally counter further down this page.
  • Elijah Can Speak open on the S sound for the "look inside" step.

Ground rules

  • Model, don't correct. If he says "thun", you say "sun, that's the S sound" and move on. Never say "no" or make him repeat more than twice.
  • Praise the tongue, not the child. "Your tongue stayed in!" beats "good boy". Be specific every time.
  • Only practise the target level. Do not correct his /s/ in ordinary chat yet. Conversation is the last rung, months away.
  • Stop early if he's tired. Ten good minutes beat thirty grumpy ones. Frustration teaches avoidance.
  • Tally honestly. "Nearly" counts as not yet. The 80% rule protects him from moving up too soon.

The session, minute by minute

Timings are a guide. If a block goes well, stay in it and bank more tries. If a block flops, drop back one rung rather than pushing forward.

0–3warm up

S ears

Aim: he can hear the difference before he has to make it

Sit side by side. You say a word; he shows a wiggly finger if he hears sss and a thumbs up if he hears th. Mix the minimal pairs below. Then swap: he chooses a card and you say it either right or wrong and he catches you. Kids love catching a parent out, and it's the same skill.

SayListen with your S ears. Is this a S word or a thumb word… sun? … thumb? … mouse? … mouth?
3–6look

Where does the tongue go?

Aim: he can point to the ridge and put his tongue in the right place with no sound

Run the S sound in Elijah Can Speak to the "tiny gap" step and leave the cutaway on screen. Ask him to find the bumpy ridge with his tongue tip. Then: teeth together, big smile, tongue in, hold it, no sound, count to three. Repeat five times. This is placement without pressure, and he learns to feel where the tongue is rather than look for it.

SayShow me the tongue on the screen. Teeth together… big smile… tongue in… freeze! One, two, three. Your tongue stayed hidden the whole time.
6–11isolation

Long S

Aim: 10 clear sss sounds on their own · tally every one

Use the ladder below from the top. Stay on the first rung that gives a clean sound. Most children with a tongue-between-teeth /s/ get it fastest from the "long t".

  1. Long t. Say "t… t… t…" with the tongue tip tapping the ridge, then let the last one leak: "t-t-t-tsssss". The tongue is already in the right place for t, so the s that follows starts from the right place too.
  2. Tongue fence. Bite gently on the coffee stirrer across the front teeth. The tongue physically cannot poke out. Blow the S past the stick. Then take the stick away and try to keep the same feeling.
  3. Straw. Hold the straw between the teeth (not on the tongue). Blow sss through it so the air has one narrow path. Then the same without the straw.
  4. Hand test. His hand in front of your mouth: sss feels cold and thin, th feels warm and wide. Now his hand in front of his own mouth. He can check himself without you saying anything.
  5. Smile and hiss. Only once the sound comes: big smile first, then the S. Rounded lips push the tongue forward, so the smile is protective.
Gate: 8 of 10 clean → syllables. Fewer → stay here, finish with the game, end on a success.
11–17syllables

S plus a vowel

Aim: 20 tries · s before and after a vowel

Start with a long S and glue the vowel on the end: "ssssss-ee", then shorten to "see". Do the vowels that keep the mouth smiling first (ee, ay), then the round ones (oo, oh), which pull the tongue forward and are harder. Then the vowel first: "eeee-sss", "ay-sss". Move the game piece every five.

sss-eesss-aysss-ahsss-ohsss-ooee-sssay-sssah-sssoh-sssoo-sss
SayLong S first, then the vowel jumps on. Ssssss… ee. Again, shorter. See! Your tongue stayed in for both.
Gate: 16 of 20 → words. Otherwise finish the session here.
17–23words

S at the start of a word

Aim: 30 tries · 10 words × 3, from the bank below

Words with /s/ at the start only, and no second /s/ or /th/ hiding inside. Say it long first ("sssss-un"), then normal. Play memory pairs or hide the cards around the room; each card found is three says. When a "thun" slips in, go back to the hand test for one try: cold thin air is the S.

SayS word: sssss-un. Sun. One more: sun. Three S sounds, all hidden. Go and find the next card!
Gate: 24 of 30 across two sessions → phrases. Final-position words (bus, house) come next, then blends (st, sp, sn).
23–27phrases

I see a…

Aim: 10 tries · only if words are at 80%

The carrier phrase "I see a ___" gives two S sounds for the price of one. Use the same cards. If he only manages the first /s/, that's fine and still counts as progress; tally the target word only.

SayI see a sun. I see a sock. Your turn, pick a card.
27–30wind down

Catch the grown-up, sticker, log

Aim: end on a win

Five words where he judges you (S or th?). He will get these right, so the session ends with success. Sticker on the chart, look at the tally together, and write one line in the log: the rung you were on, the percentage, and one thing that helped. That line is what you show the therapist.

SayThirty-eight clear S sounds today and your tongue stayed in for most of them. Tomorrow we'll try the "I see" game again.

The ladder

This is the traditional articulation hierarchy. The highlighted row is where this session sits. The therapist decides when he moves; you supply the tallies.

RungHe can…Move up when
1 · Hear itTell S words from thumb words, and catch you saying them wrong.9 of 10 judgements right
2 · Place itPut teeth together, tongue in, smile, on request, silently.Reliably, five times in a row
3 · Sound aloneA long clear sss.8 of 10, two sessions running

Two weeks of practice

Little and often beats one long session. Fifteen minutes with 60 tries is a good day. Week two repeats week one at the rung he has reached.

When it goes wrong

Tongue keeps poking out

Back to the tongue fence for the whole block. Bite, blow, remove, repeat. Some children need the stick for two weeks and that is fine.

No sss at all, just air or th

Stay on "long t" and never ask for the sound in words yet. Ten clear t-t-tsss a day is the job.

A slushy, wet, sideways sound

That is a different pattern (air leaking over the sides of the tongue). Don't drill it; tell the therapist, because the cues are different.

Perfect in words, gone in chat

Completely normal. Words to conversation is the longest stretch of all. Keep the level, add phrases, and don't correct him at dinner.

He's fed up

Stop, do the "catch the grown-up" game so it ends on a win, and try a shorter session tomorrow. Never end on a failed try.

Nasal, stuffy-sounding speech

Not a tongue thing. Mention it to the therapist and ask whether an ear, nose and throat check for adenoids or a tongue-tie assessment is worth doing.

For the therapist

This plan (for Elijah, used with the Elijah Can Speak app) uses the traditional articulation approach (ear training → placement → isolation → syllables → words → phrases → sentences → conversation) with an 80% criterion, five-count reinforcement and a stick or straw to block interdental placement. Target /s/ is taught tip-down; switch the cue card to tip-up if that is your target. We will only practise the rung you set, and will bring the tally log to each appointment. Please tell us if you would rather we did not do the "S ears" contrast with the th words, or if you want a different elicitation order.

Sources the plan draws on: ASHA Practice Portal, Speech Sound Disorders: Articulation and Phonology (traditional and contextual approaches); RCSLT guidance on speech sound difficulties; Van Riper's traditional articulation hierarchy; Secord et al., Eliciting Sounds (the "long t" and stirrer techniques); Caroline Bowen's free word lists at speech-language-therapy.com.

This is a parent's practice plan, not a clinical programme. Anything here can be overruled by his therapist, and should be.
